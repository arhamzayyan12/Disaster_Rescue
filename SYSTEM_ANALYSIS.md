# Chapter 3: System Analysis

## 3.1 Introduction

System analysis is the foundational phase of any software engineering project, concerned with understanding the domain, identifying the problems to be solved, specifying requirements, and establishing the boundaries of the proposed system before implementation begins. For the Disaster Evacuation Management System (DEMS), this phase involved a thorough investigation of disaster management workflows in the Indian context, a study of the government data sources and protocols that govern emergency response, and a structured identification of the technical, operational, and user-facing requirements that the system must satisfy. The analysis drew from real-world constraints imposed by India's National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA), the characteristics of open satellite data from NASA, and the limitations of existing public alerting infrastructure. This chapter presents the outcomes of that analysis in six parts: problem definition, feasibility study, functional and non-functional requirement specification, use case descriptions, data flow analysis, and system constraints.

---

## 3.2 Problem Definition

Natural and man-made disasters pose an enormous and recurring threat to human life, property, and infrastructure in India. Each year, millions of citizens are affected by floods, cyclones, earthquakes, heatwaves, wildfires, and landslides. Despite the existence of government agencies such as the National Disaster Management Authority and state-level counterparts, the practical dissemination of disaster information and the coordination of evacuation logistics remain deeply fragmented in practice. A careful study of this problem space revealed six distinct and compounding deficiencies that the proposed system seeks to resolve.

The first and most pervasive problem is the absence of a unified information platform. Disaster data is currently scattered across multiple siloed sources, including the NDMA SACHET portal, India Meteorological Department weather feeds, NASA satellite detection systems, and various state emergency portals. No single, integrated access point exists for either the public or for emergency responders to view the complete and current disaster picture. The second problem is the delay in public awareness. Official alert broadcasts via SMS or television typically reach affected populations slowly and, critically, without actionable guidance — citizens are informed that a cyclone is approaching but are not directed to the nearest operational shelter or guided along an evacuation route.

The third problem concerns the manual nature of relief coordination. Relief centre administrators currently rely on telephone calls, paper registers, and unofficial messaging channels to track shelter occupancy, relief inventory levels, and the status of evacuees. This approach is error-prone, slow, and difficult to coordinate across multiple simultaneously active relief camps. The fourth problem is the absence of real-time wildfire monitoring in any citizen-facing Indian application. Active fire detections from satellite data are publicly available through NASA's Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS), yet no accessible platform integrates this data for public or responder use at the time of this project.

The fifth problem relates to evacuee identity tracking during mass evacuations. No efficient mechanism currently exists to digitally log the arrival of individuals at evacuation shelters, record their medical requirements, or confirm their safe status — which results in delayed family reunification and misallocation of relief resources. The sixth and final identified problem is the absence of standardized, NDMA-compliant shelter assessment. The selection of shelters during a disaster is often ad hoc, without systematic evaluation against NDMA criteria such as minimum floor area per displaced person, sanitation facility ratios, and daily water supply adequacy. The proposed DEMS directly addresses each of these six problem dimensions through its integrated, real-time, cloud-backed architecture.

## 3.3 Feasibility Analysis

### 3.3.1 Technical Feasibility

The technical feasibility of the proposed system was assessed by evaluating whether the required capabilities could be implemented using available tools, frameworks, and external APIs. The system is built entirely using well-established, production-grade open-source technologies. The frontend is developed with React 18 and TypeScript, which are among the most widely adopted and thoroughly documented frameworks in modern web development. Interactive mapping is provided through Leaflet.js and React-Leaflet, which are capable, browser-native mapping libraries requiring no commercial GIS licensing. The backend data layer is powered by Supabase, a hosted PostgreSQL service with a free tier that satisfies the storage and concurrency requirements of the project. All external data sources consumed by the system — including the NDMA SACHET RSS feed, the NASA FIRMS API, and the OpenStreetMap Overpass API — are publicly accessible at no cost. No proprietary software, paid cloud services, or specialized hardware is required at any layer of the system, confirming its technical feasibility.

### 3.3.2 Operational Feasibility

The system was designed to be accessible through a standard web browser on any device, including mobile phones, without requiring a dedicated application download or installation. This approach maximizes reach across both technically literate administrators and general citizens affected by a disaster. The user interface exposes two distinct roles — administrator and citizen — each with appropriately scoped views and permissions, ensuring that the system aligns with the organizational hierarchy of existing disaster management agencies without requiring them to adopt fundamentally new procedures. The interface's modular tab-based navigation (Map, Relief, Analytics, News, Guidelines) follows familiar web application conventions, reducing the learning curve for first-time users in emergency conditions. Operational feasibility is therefore confirmed.

### 3.3.3 Economic Feasibility

All software components used in the development and deployment of DEMS are available under open-source licenses or free-tier cloud plans. The Supabase free tier supports up to 500 MB of storage and 50,000 monthly active users, which is ample for the scale of a regional pilot deployment. Vercel Analytics, used for deployment monitoring and performance tracking, is available under a no-cost plan. The NASA FIRMS API requires only a free API key obtained through registration on the NASA Earthdata portal. There are no licensing fees, server procurement costs, database subscription charges, or recurring operational expenses required to deploy and maintain the system, making DEMS economically feasible for government agencies, non-governmental organizations, and academic research institutions alike.

### 3.3.4 Schedule Feasibility

The system was designed and developed within the academic project timeline of one semester. The modular architecture, which clearly separates concerns into distinct layers for services, components, state management, types, and utilities, enabled parallel development of independent subsystems. The disaster map, relief dashboard, analytics engine, and QR code modules were developed concurrently by working on isolated files and integrating them through clearly defined TypeScript interfaces. This architecture ensured that the project remained within schedule constraints despite the breadth of its feature set.

## 3.4 Functional Requirements

Functional requirements describe the specific behaviours and capabilities the system must provide to its users and dependent systems. The following requirements were identified through domain analysis, study of existing disaster management protocols, and iterative design of the system architecture.

The first functional requirement (FR-01) mandates real-time disaster data ingestion from the official NDMA SACHET RSS feed. The system fetches alerts from the SACHET endpoint at regular intervals, parsing each item from its CAP (Common Alerting Protocol) XML format. The parsing process extracts the disaster type, severity level, geographic coordinates or area description, alert description text, and expiry timestamp. Location disambiguation is performed using natural language matching against a curated database of Indian cities and states defined in the `constants/locations.ts` module.

The second requirement (FR-02) specifies satellite wildfire detection and persistent storage. On application load and at subsequent 15-minute intervals, the `FirmsIngestionService` queries the NASA FIRMS API using the VIIRS SNPP Near Real-Time sensor over India's geographic bounding box. The returned CSV data is parsed field by field, and individual fire detection points are spatially and temporally clustered using a Haversine-distance algorithm with a 5-kilometre radius and a 30-minute time window. Each resulting unique fire cluster is stored in the Supabase `disasters` table with the source attribute `NASA_FIRMS_VIIRS`.

The third requirement (FR-03) addresses intelligent deduplication of the combined disaster dataset. Because NDMA alerts and FIRMS detections may both report the same fire event using different data models and coordinate precision, the `fetchAllDisasters()` function applies a dual-priority merging algorithm. NDMA-sourced alerts are treated as authoritative and take precedence over FIRMS detections that fall within a 0.03-degree proximity threshold. More broadly, any two records of the same disaster type within a 0.05-degree radius and a 4-hour time window are considered duplicates, with only the higher-priority or earlier-indexed record retained.

The fourth requirement (FR-04) specifies an interactive disaster map rendered through Leaflet.js and React-Leaflet within the `DisasterMap.tsx` component. All active disaster events are displayed as colour-coded, type-specific markers, with marker clustering enabled via `react-leaflet-cluster` to manage visual density at lower zoom levels. The map supports four independently toggleable layers: Disasters, Shelters, Wildfires, and Weather. Selecting any marker reveals a side panel (`DisasterDetailsPanel.tsx`) showing the full event details.

The fifth and sixth requirements (FR-05 and FR-06) together specify geolocation-based shelter discovery with NDMA compliance scoring. When a user navigates to the Relief section and supplies an affected population count, the `shelter-service.ts` module queries the OpenStreetMap Overpass API for hospitals, schools, community centres, and emergency shelters within a configurable radius of the user's GPS location. Each retrieved facility is evaluated against NDMA standards, including a minimum area of 3.5 square metres per person, a water supply of 15 litres per person per day, specified toilet ratios, and caloric intake adequacy. The compliance evaluation is performed by the `ndma-protocol-service.ts` module, which returns a numeric compliance score and a list of warnings for each facility. A strategic score combining compliance weight (60%), proximity (20%), and facility type priority (20%) is then used to rank and present the top 15 candidate shelters.

The seventh requirement (FR-07) defines a relief coordination dashboard for administrative users. Authenticated administrators can create and update relief camp records through the `ReliefDashboard.tsx` interface, managing inventory levels for food, water, and medicine, as well as tracking current occupancy and contact information for each camp. All data changes are persisted to Supabase and immediately propagated to all connected clients via the real-time subscription mechanism.

The eighth requirement (FR-08) specifies a QR code-based evacuee identification system. Each registered evacuee profile generates a unique QR code through the `QRCodeDisplayModal.tsx` component, encoding identity, shelter assignment, and medical flag data. Emergency personnel at relief checkpoints can upload a QR image through `QRCodeUpload.tsx`, which decodes it to instantly retrieve the evacuee's information, facilitating rapid check-ins without network-intensive database lookups.

The ninth through thirteenth requirements cover supporting system capabilities: a live disaster news feed that links articles to specific map-located events (FR-09); a multi-panel analytics dashboard showing frequency distribution, severity charts, monthly trends, and geographic heatmaps derived from live disaster data (FR-10); a structured safety and evacuation guidelines module organized by disaster type and sourced from NDMA standards (FR-11); a role-based authentication system via Supabase Auth distinguishing administrator and citizen access levels (FR-12); and a WebSocket-based real-time subscription to the Supabase `disasters` table that triggers automatic client-side updates upon any database change event (FR-13).

## 3.5 Non-Functional Requirements

Non-functional requirements define the quality attributes and operational characteristics the system must exhibit in addition to its functional behaviour. These requirements are equally important in determining the suitability of the system for real-world deployment under emergency conditions.

In terms of performance, the application is designed to achieve an initial page load within three seconds on a standard broadband connection. This is achieved through code-splitting and lazy loading of computationally heavy components — including `ReliefDashboard`, `DisasterAnalytics`, `LiveNews`, and `SafetyGuidelines` — using React's `Suspense` API, which defers their loading until first accessed by the user.

For reliability, the system is designed to degrade gracefully under API failure conditions. If the NDMA SACHET or NASA FIRMS API calls fail, the system falls back to data stored in the Supabase `disasters` table without crashing. Component-level error boundaries implemented in `ErrorBoundary.tsx` prevent failures in one interface section from propagating to and disabling the remainder of the application.

Concerning scalability, the Supabase PostgreSQL schema creates composite indexes on the `(lat, lng)` column pair and the `reported_at` column, enabling efficient geospatial proximity queries as the number of stored disaster records grows over time. The NDMA-sourced alerts are not persisted but are computed fresh on each fetch cycle, keeping the stored dataset focused on persistent satellite data.

With respect to security, all database operations are mediated through Supabase Row Level Security policies. Public read access to the `disasters` table is explicitly granted, while write access is restricted to authenticated sessions. API keys for NASA FIRMS and Supabase are stored exclusively in environment variables loaded at build time and are never embedded in client-side source code. The `.env` file is excluded from version control via `.gitignore`.

The usability requirement is addressed through a responsive CSS layout that adapts across desktop and mobile viewports, and through the `BottomNav.tsx` component that provides a thumb-friendly mobile navigation bar replacing the top header tabs on small screens. The system's intent is to remain operable under the reduced cognitive capacity characteristic of users during disaster stress.

Regarding maintainability, the codebase is organized into clearly separated directories for components, services, types, utilities, contexts, and hooks. All external data integrations are encapsulated exclusively within the `services` layer, ensuring that changes to third-party API contracts can be addressed in isolation without modifying component logic. Data freshness is maintained through a 10-minute polling interval for disaster records and a 15-minute FIRMS ingestion cycle, with the latter protected by a localStorage-based anti-spam cooldown to prevent excessive API calls across browser tabs and page reloads.

## 3.6 System Actors and Use Cases

### 3.6.1 Identified Actors

The system interacts with four distinct categories of actors. The first is the Citizen or Evacuee — a member of the general public who accesses the system during or in anticipation of a disaster event, primarily to view the disaster map, locate shelters, read safety guidelines, and register an evacuee profile. The second is the Administrator — a government official, NGO coordinator, or emergency response manager who authenticates with administrative credentials to manage relief camps, update disaster records, and monitor relief center inventories. The third actor is a set of External APIs, comprising the NDMA SACHET RSS feed, NASA FIRMS satellite detection API, and OpenStreetMap Overpass API, which function as automated data providers that continuously supply the system with environmental intelligence. The fourth actor is Supabase, the cloud-hosted backend that manages persistent data storage, real-time event subscriptions, and access-controlled authentication for the system.

### 3.6.2 Core Use Case Descriptions

The primary use case is viewing the disaster map. A citizen opens the application, which automatically fetches current disaster alerts from NDMA SACHET and merges them with satellite wildfire detections from Supabase. The map renders with colour-coded markers categorised by disaster type. The user can pan and zoom freely, toggle visibility of different data layers, and click any marker to open a detail panel displaying the disaster's type, severity, description, reported time, and geographic location. If any API call fails, the system transparently falls back to recently stored data.

The second key use case is finding the nearest shelter. A citizen with geolocation enabled navigates to the Relief Section and enters the number of people needing shelter. The system reads the browser's GPS coordinates and queries the Overpass API for suitable facilities within the configured radius. Each facility is scored against NDMA standards and ranked. The top fifteen results are displayed on the Relief Map with colour-coded compliance indicators, enabling the user to select the most suitable shelter for their group.

The third use case is the automated ingestion of wildfire data, which occurs without user intervention. On application startup and every fifteen minutes thereafter, the `FirmsIngestionService` polls the NASA FIRMS API for fire hotspot detections over India, clusters the results, deduplicates against the database, and inserts new wildfire events. This process runs entirely in the background and is subject to a throttling mechanism to prevent API rate limit breaches.

The fourth use case covers administrative relief camp management. An authenticated administrator navigates to the Relief Dashboard, where they can add new relief centres, update inventory counts, manage occupancy figures, and record contact information. All changes are reflected in the Supabase database and are immediately visible to field personnel accessing the same dashboard.

The fifth use case is QR code-based evacuee registration and verification. An evacuee presents their QR code — generated upon profile registration — at a relief checkpoint. An emergency officer uploads the QR image to the system, which decodes it and retrieves the evacuee's identity, assigned shelter, and any flagged medical conditions, enabling fast, reliable check-in without reliance on verbal communication or network-intensive record lookups.

## 3.7 Data Flow Analysis

### 3.7.1 Disaster Ingestion Pipeline

The primary data pipeline originates from two separate external sources — the NDMA SACHET RSS feed and the NASA FIRMS satellite API — and converges within the application before being delivered to the user interface. For the SACHET pathway, the `disaster-data-service.ts` module issues an HTTP GET request to the SACHET endpoint, with the request routed through a Vite development proxy to circumvent CORS restrictions. The returned XML is parsed using the browser's native DOMParser, after which each RSS channel item is inspected for embedded CAP alert elements or analysed through keyword-based natural language processing. City and state names mentioned in alert text are matched against the `indianCities` dictionary to resolve geographic coordinates. The resulting objects are typed against the `Disaster` interface and filtered to retain only alerts from the current day or with a future expiry timestamp.

For the FIRMS pathway, the `FirmsIngestionService` fetches comma-separated fire detection data containing latitude, longitude, fire radiative power, acquisition time, and confidence values. Each row is parsed and converted to a structured object with a normalised ISO timestamp. The clustering algorithm groups nearby detections — those within five kilometres and thirty minutes of each other — into single representative fire events. Each cluster is compared against existing Supabase records using a bounding-box proximity query before being inserted as a new disaster record with source attribution.

The `fetchAllDisasters()` function then combines both datasets, applies the deduplication logic that grants precedence to official NDMA alerts over FIRMS detections where they overlap geographically, and returns the final unified array to the React application state. A Supabase real-time channel subscription running in parallel ensures that any change to the `disasters` table — including new FIRMS insertions from another browser tab — triggers a fresh call to `fetchAllDisasters()`, keeping all open sessions synchronized without manual refresh.

### 3.7.2 Shelter Discovery Pipeline

The second significant data pipeline begins when a user requests shelter recommendations. The application reads the current GPS coordinates from the browser's Geolocation API, defaulting to the geographic centroid of India if access is denied. The `fetchShelters()` function in `shelter-service.ts` constructs an Overpass QL query targeting nodes, ways, and relations tagged as hospitals, schools, community centres, social facilities, or emergency shelters within the specified search radius. The Overpass API response is parsed into raw shelter objects, each enriched with an estimated floor area based on facility type. The `calculateNDMACompliance()` function from `ndma-protocol-service.ts` then evaluates each facility against the NDMA shelter guidelines, computing a compliance score and a list of specific standard violations. A composite strategic score is calculated for each facility, weighting compliance at 60%, proximity at 20%, and facility type priority at 20%. The results are sorted in descending order of this score, and the top fifteen are returned for display on the `ReliefMap.tsx` component.

## 3.8 System Constraints

The analysis phase also surfaced a set of technical and environmental constraints that directly influenced the design of the system. Understanding these constraints is essential for interpreting certain architectural decisions made during implementation.

The most significant constraint encountered was the CORS policy enforced by the NDMA SACHET server, which does not include the HTTP headers necessary for browser-based JavaScript requests to access the RSS feed directly. This necessitated the configuration of a Vite development server proxy that routes requests to `/api/sachet` through to the SACHET endpoint, bypassing the browser's CORS enforcement during development. For production deployment, the system falls back to a public CORS proxy service, though this introduces a dependency on a third-party intermediary that may not always be available.

The NASA FIRMS API enforces rate limits that prevent excessively frequent polling. To respect these limits, the `FirmsIngestionService` stores the timestamp of the last successful fetch call in the browser's `localStorage`. Any polling attempt within ten minutes of the last fetch is immediately aborted, regardless of how many browser tabs or application instances are open simultaneously. This constraint also influenced the decision to persist FIRMS data in Supabase rather than re-fetching it on every page load — once stored, wildfire events are available from the database without needing to re-contact the NASA API.

The geolocation-based shelter discovery feature is constrained by the availability and accuracy of the browser's Geolocation API. If the user's device does not support geolocation, if the user declines the browser's location permission request, or if the GPS signal is unavailable, the system defaults to the centroid of India as the search origin. This fallback ensures the feature remains functional but produces results centred on central India rather than the user's actual location.

The NLP-based location extraction used to geolocate NDMA alerts is constrained by the coverage of the city dictionary defined in `constants/locations.ts`. Alerts that mention geographic areas not included in this dictionary — such as small towns, tehsils, or newly named administrative units — cannot be precisely located and may fall back to state-level coordinates or be discarded if no valid location can be resolved. Expanding this dictionary represents a straightforward path to improving alert coverage in future system iterations.

Finally, the system does not currently implement offline operation. Map tiles, disaster data, and all dynamic content require an active internet connection. In the event of complete network failure — which is a realistic scenario during major disasters that damage telecommunications infrastructure — the system becomes inoperable. Implementing a service worker-based caching strategy to persist recent disaster and shelter data for offline access is identified as a priority for future enhancement.

## 3.9 Summary

This chapter has comprehensively established the analytical foundation for the Disaster Evacuation Management System. The problem space was defined across six specific dimensions, each rooted in observable deficiencies in the current Indian disaster management ecosystem. Feasibility was evaluated and confirmed across technical, operational, economic, and schedule dimensions. Thirteen functional requirements capturing the full behavioural scope of the system were formally specified, alongside seven non-functional requirements governing performance, reliability, scalability, security, usability, maintainability, and data freshness. Four system actors and five core use cases were described in detail, followed by a narrative data flow analysis of both the disaster ingestion pipeline and the shelter discovery pipeline. The chapter concluded with a transparent discussion of five system constraints that shaped key architectural decisions. The System Design chapter that follows proceeds from this analysis to define the structural and implementation decisions through which these requirements are realised.
